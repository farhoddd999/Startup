The science

Built on how people really learn to read.

Nothing in Hearth is new science. It brings together methods with good evidence behind them (phonics, stories made only of words you can read, talking about the story together) and shapes them for grown-ups, many of whom have felt embarrassed about reading for years.

Principles

Six ideas we keep coming back to.

One sound at a time

Forty small steps, starting with m, s, t and the short a in “cat”, and ending with longer word endings like -tion. Each step adds a few new letter sounds and practises them in real words. This is called structured phonics.

Every word is readable

A parent only gets a story if every word in it is one they can sound out, a heart word they already know by sight, or their own family’s names. Reading people call this “decodable” text.

The smallest bit of help first

First the parts of the word, then the sounds with pictures, and only then the word itself. The reader does as much of the work as they can, because that’s how sounding out gets easier.

Practise, then read for real

Reading the same text more than once is one of the best ways to become a smoother reader. Practising first and then reading the same story at bedtime gives that repetition a reason the parent really cares about.

Talking about the story

A question on every page turns the story into a conversation. When researchers pooled studies of this kind of shared reading (“dialogic reading”), they found a moderate boost to the words children use (d = 0.59).7

No one should feel tested

There are no levels, scores or red marks, and you’ll never see words like “illiterate” or “beginner”. Embarrassment is what keeps most adults from getting help, so we’ve taken out anything that feels like a test.

How we know a word is readable

Every word, broken into its sounds.

For each word, Hearth looks up how it’s said (in the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary) and matches the letters to the sounds using 214 spelling rules, picking the simplest match. That tells us exactly which letter sounds someone needs to know to read it.

Why it adds up

Small steps, big jumps.

Learning blends like “fr” and “st” at step 18 opens up more than 300 common words in one go. Word endings and longer words at steps 34 and 35 open up over a thousand. This is the number Hearth shows parents, instead of a level.

Keeping track of progress

Knowing what each parent already knows.

For every parent, Hearth keeps a private guess at how well they know each of the 120 letter-sound patterns. That guess lives on their phone and nowhere else. (For the technically minded: it’s Bayesian knowledge tracing, adjusted for adults.)

It learns from your reading

A word read smoothly counts in favour of every sound in it. A word that needed help only counts against the sound you know least.

Learned over days, not minutes

A sound only counts as learned after you’ve read it well on two different days, so one good evening isn’t mistaken for the real thing.

Forgiving

One tired night doesn’t undo your progress, and skipping a page never counts against you.

It picks tonight’s story

The first sound you’re still learning decides which story you get tonight, and which new sound it practises.

Finding out if it works

We’ll measure two learners, not one.

Hearth works, but we don’t yet know how much it helps. Our first pilot is set up to find out what it does for parents and for their children, and we’ll share the results even if they’re not what we hoped.
